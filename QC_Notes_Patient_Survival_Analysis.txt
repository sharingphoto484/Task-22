QC NOTES FOR PATIENT SURVIVAL PREDICTION ANALYSIS

PROMPT GOAL IN 5 SENTENCES:

The analysis aims to build comprehensive patient survival prediction models using three healthcare datasets containing patient demographics, medical conditions, and binary health indicators to predict one year survival outcomes. Multiple advanced supervised learning algorithms including logistic regression with L2 regularization, random forest for feature ranking, gradient boosting with cross validation, support vector machines with kernel methods, and k nearest neighbors are required to be implemented with specific hyperparameters. Each modeling technique must produce specific quantitative outputs such as standardized coefficients, feature importance scores, cross validated AUC metrics, predicted probabilities, and demographic statistics to evaluate model performance. The training dataset with 25079 labeled patient records is used for model development while two testing datasets with 9330 and 9303 unlabeled records respectively are used for prediction evaluation at different difficulty levels. The ultimate objective is to identify the most critical risk factors affecting patient survival and determine the optimal modeling strategy that maximizes prediction accuracy based on feature importance rankings and ROC AUC performance metrics.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTIONS:

Step 1: The analysis begins by loading three Excel datasets into pandas dataframes representing the training set with survival outcomes and two testing sets without labels for prediction tasks. The training dataset contains 25079 patient records with 18 columns including demographic features, medical conditions, binary health indicators, and the target variable indicating one year survival status. Both testing datasets contain identical feature structures with 17 columns excluding the survival outcome variable and are designated for intermediate and advanced difficulty prediction assessments.

Step 2: For logistic regression analysis, data preprocessing involves selecting only those patient records where the survival outcome, patient age, patient body mass index, and all six binary health indicators labeled A through F have no missing values. This data cleaning step ensures model training occurs on complete cases without any null values that could interfere with the standardization process or coefficient estimation. The resulting cleaned dataset maintains data integrity required for reliable logistic regression modeling with L2 regularization.

Step 3: Continuous numeric features specifically patient age and patient body mass index undergo standardization transformation to achieve zero mean and unit variance using sklearn StandardScaler. This standardization process is critical because it places both continuous features on the same scale allowing fair comparison of their regression coefficients in the final model. The six binary health indicators A through F remain unchanged as they are already on a zero to one scale and do not require standardization.

Step 4: A logistic regression model with L2 penalty regularization is instantiated with regularization parameter C set to one and random state set to forty two for reproducibility of results across multiple runs. The model is trained using the standardized continuous features and original binary indicators as eight predictor variables with the survived one year variable as the binary target outcome. L2 regularization helps prevent overfitting by penalizing large coefficient values and improves model generalization to unseen patient data.

Step 5: After model training completes, the coefficient corresponding to the standardized patient age feature is extracted from the fitted logistic regression model coefficient array. This coefficient value of negative 0.1745 indicates that increasing patient age is associated with decreased probability of one year survival when holding all other predictors constant. The negative sign and magnitude of this standardized coefficient quantifies the directional impact and relative importance of age in predicting patient survival outcomes.

Step 6: Random forest feature importance analysis requires a broader set of ten predictor variables including patient age, patient body mass index, diagnosed condition, number of previous conditions, and the six binary health indicators A through F. Complete case analysis is performed by removing any patient records with missing values across these ten features or the survival outcome variable. This comprehensive feature set allows the random forest algorithm to evaluate both continuous demographic variables and categorical medical condition indicators for their predictive power.

Step 7: A random forest classifier is configured with one hundred decision trees, maximum tree depth limited to ten levels, and random state forty two for result reproducibility. The model is trained on the complete case dataset where each decision tree learns different patterns from bootstrap samples of the training data. The ensemble of one hundred trees aggregates predictions through majority voting and calculates feature importance scores based on mean decrease in node impurity across all trees.

Step 8: Feature importance scores are extracted from the trained random forest model where each score represents how much each predictor variable contributes to reducing prediction uncertainty. Patient body mass index emerges as the most important feature with an importance score of 0.4062 indicating it provides the strongest signal for discriminating between patients who survive versus those who do not. This feature importance ranking guides subsequent modeling decisions by identifying which variables deserve primary focus in prediction strategies.

Step 9: Gradient boosting implementation using XGBoost requires selecting complete patient records with eight features comprising patient age, patient body mass index, and the six binary health indicators A through F. The XGBoost classifier is configured with learning rate of 0.1 controlling the contribution of each tree, maximum depth of five limiting tree complexity, and one hundred boosting iterations building sequential trees. Random state forty two ensures consistent results and eval metric is set to logloss for binary classification optimization.

Step 10: Five fold cross validation is performed where the training data is split into five equal parts and the model is trained five separate times using four folds for training and one fold for validation in rotation. For each of the five iterations, the area under ROC curve is calculated on the held out validation fold measuring the model ability to discriminate between survival outcomes. The mean AUC across all five folds equals 0.7413 demonstrating strong discriminative ability and indicating the gradient boosting model generalizes well beyond the training data.

Step 11: Support vector machine classification requires training a model on the complete training dataset and then applying it to the advanced testing dataset for survival probability predictions. An SVM with radial basis function kernel is configured with gamma parameter 0.1 controlling kernel width, regularization parameter C equal to one balancing margin maximization with classification errors, and probability estimation enabled. Training features undergo standardization using the mean and standard deviation computed from the training set to ensure consistent scaling between training and testing phases.

Step 12: The trained SVM model with fitted parameters is applied to the testing advance dataset after standardizing test features using the training set statistics to maintain scale consistency. Predicted survival probabilities are generated for each test patient using the predict proba method which outputs probability estimates for both survival classes. The mean predicted survival probability across all 9330 test patients equals 0.6209 indicating that on average the model predicts approximately sixty two percent chance of one year survival for the advanced test population.

Step 13: K nearest neighbors classification uses a different feature set including patient age, patient body mass index, diagnosed condition, and six binary indicators standardized to zero mean unit variance. A KNN classifier with k equals five neighbors and Euclidean distance metric is trained on the complete training dataset where predictions are made by majority voting among the five nearest training examples. The fitted model is then applied to the intermediate testing dataset to predict survival outcomes for 9303 unlabeled patient records.

Step 14: The proportion of positive survival predictions is calculated by counting how many test patients received a prediction of one indicating survival and dividing by the total number of test observations. This predicted positive rate of 66.24 percent represents the percentage of intermediate test patients that the KNN model classifies as likely to survive one year. The higher positive rate compared to SVM predictions suggests the KNN model may be more optimistic or the intermediate test population may have more favorable survival characteristics.

Step 15: ROC curve analysis requires splitting the training data into eighty percent for model training and twenty percent for validation using stratified random sampling with random state forty two. A logistic regression model is trained on the training split and then generates survival probability predictions for the validation split patients. The ROC curve plots true positive rate against false positive rate at various probability thresholds and the area under this curve equals 0.6200 quantifying the model discrimination ability on held out validation data.

Step 16: Demographic distribution analysis focuses on the testing advance dataset by grouping patients according to their smoking status category after removing records with missing smoker information. For each smoking status group, the number of patients and mean age are calculated to understand the distribution of smoking habits in the test population. The ratio of maximum group count to minimum group count equals 263.27 revealing substantial imbalance in smoking status categories where some categories have far more patients than others.

Step 17: Feature correlation analysis examines the linear relationship between patient age and patient body mass index in the intermediate testing dataset using Pearson correlation coefficient. Complete cases with both age and BMI values are selected and the correlation is computed measuring the strength and direction of their linear association. The resulting correlation coefficient of 0.0032 indicates virtually no linear relationship between age and BMI in this patient population suggesting these two demographic variables provide independent information.

Step 18: Age distribution comparison involves calculating the mean age of patients who survived in the training dataset which equals 32.06 years representing the average age of one year survivors. The overall mean age in the intermediate testing dataset is calculated as 33.25 years representing the average age of all test patients regardless of unknown survival status. The absolute difference of 1.19 years between these two means suggests the testing population is slightly older on average compared to training set survivors.

Step 19: The modeling strategy recommendation synthesizes findings from random forest feature importance analysis showing patient body mass index as the top predictor and the cross validated AUC scores demonstrating strong model performance. Ensemble methods like gradient boosting achieve the highest discriminative ability with mean AUC of 0.7413 outperforming individual logistic regression validation AUC of 0.6200. The optimal strategy prioritizes ensemble approaches combined with feature selection guided by importance rankings, cross validation for robust performance estimation, and probability calibration to maximize prediction accuracy while preventing overfitting.

Step 20: All nine analytical tasks produce quantitative outputs including standardized coefficients, feature importance scores, cross validated metrics, predicted probabilities, demographic statistics, and correlation measures that collectively characterize patient survival patterns. The analysis demonstrates that body mass index is the most critical predictor, age has negative impact on survival, and ensemble methods provide superior discriminative performance. These findings enable healthcare practitioners to focus on key risk factors and select appropriate modeling techniques for accurate patient survival prediction in clinical decision support systems.
