QUALITY CONTROL NOTES - PATIENT SURVIVAL PREDICTION ANALYSIS

PROMPT GOAL IN 5 SENTENCES

The primary objective is to build a comprehensive patient survival prediction system using advanced supervised learning techniques on three healthcare datasets containing patient demographics, clinical indicators, and treatment information. The analysis requires implementing six distinct machine learning models including logistic regression with L2 regularization, random forest for feature importance ranking, gradient boosting with cross validation, support vector machines with kernel methods, k nearest neighbors classification, and ROC curve analysis for performance evaluation. Each model targets specific analytical questions such as extracting age coefficients, identifying most predictive features, calculating cross validated AUC scores, predicting survival probabilities on test sets, and computing demographic distributions and correlations. The datasets comprise Training set advance with 25079 labeled patient records, Testing set advance with 9330 unlabeled records, and Testing set intermediate with 9303 unlabeled records, all containing patient age, body mass index, six binary condition indicators A through F, diagnosed conditions, and previous condition counts. The ultimate goal is to determine the optimal modeling strategy that maximizes prediction accuracy by combining insights from feature importance analysis and ensemble learning performance metrics.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTIONS

Step 1: The analysis begins with generating three synthetic patient survival datasets that replicate real world healthcare data patterns with controlled statistical properties. Training set advance contains 25079 patient records with complete feature sets including patient identification, age ranging from 18 to 95 years, body mass index, smoking status, rural urban location, mental condition, six binary health indicators labeled A through F, Z indicator, number of previous conditions, and the target variable Survived 1 year representing one year survival outcome. Missing values are intentionally introduced at a 2 percent rate in continuous and binary features to simulate realistic data collection scenarios where some patient information may be incomplete or unavailable.

Step 2: Testing set advance is created with 9330 patient records containing identical feature structure as training data but excluding the survival outcome variable since this represents unlabeled data for model prediction evaluation. The demographic distributions in testing advance are slightly shifted compared to training data with mean age of 56 versus 55 and smoking status proportions adjusted to 58 percent non smokers, 27 percent former smokers, and 15 percent current smokers. Missing values are introduced at a lower 1.5 percent rate to represent higher quality test data that might be available in clinical validation scenarios.

Step 3: Testing set intermediate is generated with 9303 patient records following the same feature structure but with different demographic characteristics including mean age of 54 and body mass index of 26.8 to represent a distinct patient population. This intermediate difficulty test set allows assessment of model generalization across patient cohorts with varying baseline characteristics. The three datasets are saved as Excel files to maintain compatibility with healthcare data systems and enable easy inspection of individual patient records.

Step 4: The first analysis implements logistic regression with L2 regularization to quantify the impact of patient age on one year survival probability while controlling for other health factors. Complete records are selected by removing any rows with missing values in the eight predictor features which are patient age, body mass index, and six binary indicators A through F, along with the survival outcome variable. This data cleaning step ensures the logistic regression model receives only complete cases for reliable parameter estimation.

Step 5: Standardization is applied exclusively to the two continuous features patient age and body mass index by transforming them to zero mean and unit variance distributions using sklearn StandardScaler. This standardization ensures that coefficient magnitudes become directly comparable and that the optimization algorithm converges efficiently without being dominated by features with larger numeric scales. The six binary indicators A through F remain unstandardized since they are already on a zero to one scale.

Step 6: Logistic regression model is fitted using L2 penalty with regularization parameter C equal to 1.0 which controls the inverse of regularization strength, and random state 42 for reproducibility of results across multiple runs. The model learns optimal coefficients for all eight predictors that maximize the likelihood of observed survival outcomes in the training data. The coefficient for standardized patient age is extracted from the first position of the coefficient array and represents the change in log odds of survival for one standard deviation increase in age.

Step 7: The extracted age coefficient is negative 0.1812 indicating that higher patient age is associated with decreased survival probability after controlling for body mass index and health condition indicators. This coefficient is on the standardized scale meaning a one standard deviation increase in age approximately 15 years decreases the log odds of survival by 0.1812 units. The standardization allows fair comparison with coefficients of other predictors regardless of their original measurement units.

Step 8: The second analysis implements random forest classifier to identify which among ten patient features has the strongest predictive power for one year survival through mean decrease in impurity rankings. Complete records are selected including patient age, body mass index, diagnosed condition categorical variable, number of previous conditions, and six binary indicators A through F along with survival outcome. The diagnosed condition categorical feature is converted to numeric codes using pandas categorical encoding to make it compatible with the random forest algorithm which requires numeric inputs.

Step 9: Random forest model is trained with 100 decision trees and maximum depth of 10 levels using random state 42 for reproducible tree construction and split selection. Each tree is built on a bootstrap sample of training data and considers random subsets of features at each split point to reduce correlation between trees and improve ensemble generalization. The max depth constraint prevents individual trees from growing too deep and overfitting to training data noise.

Step 10: Feature importances are extracted from the trained random forest model where each importance score represents the total reduction in node impurity weighted by the probability of reaching that node across all trees in the forest. The feature with maximum importance score is identified as Patient Body Mass Index with an importance of 0.2565 indicating it provides the most information gain for predicting survival outcomes. This suggests that body mass index variations explain more survival outcome variance than age, diagnosed condition, previous condition count, or any of the six binary health indicators in this patient population.

Step 11: The third analysis implements XGBoost gradient boosting classifier with five fold cross validation to obtain robust estimates of model discrimination ability measured by area under ROC curve. Complete records are selected with eight features including patient age, body mass index, and binary indicators A through F along with survival outcome, excluding categorical variables to focus on numeric predictors. The dataset is automatically partitioned into five non overlapping folds where each fold serves as validation set once while the other four folds serve as training data.

Step 12: XGBoost model is configured with learning rate 0.1 controlling the contribution of each tree to the ensemble, max depth 5 limiting tree complexity, 100 estimators specifying total number of boosting rounds, and random state 42 for reproducibility. Cross validation is performed using sklearn cross val score with roc auc scoring metric which computes area under ROC curve for each of the five validation folds. The mean cross validated AUC is 0.6506 indicating moderate discrimination ability where the model correctly ranks a randomly chosen survivor higher than a randomly chosen non survivor 65.06 percent of the time.

Step 13: The fourth analysis trains a support vector machine classifier with radial basis function kernel on the training set and then applies it to Testing set advance to predict survival probabilities for unlabeled patient records. Training data preparation involves selecting complete records with eight numeric features and survival outcomes, then standardizing all features using fit transform to learn training set mean and standard deviation parameters. The SVM is fitted with RBF kernel using gamma 0.1 controlling kernel width, regularization parameter C equal to 1.0 balancing margin maximization and training error, random state 42 for reproducibility, and probability True enabling probability calibration through Platt scaling.

Step 14: Testing set advance is loaded and complete records with the eight required features are selected, then standardized using transform method with the same scaler fitted on training data to ensure consistent feature scales. This ensures test data is transformed using training statistics rather than test statistics which would constitute data leakage and produce overly optimistic results. The trained SVM model predicts survival probabilities for each test patient and the mean predicted probability across all test observations is 0.4719 indicating the model predicts an average 47.19 percent survival rate in the test population.

Step 15: The fifth analysis implements k nearest neighbors classification to predict survival outcomes on Testing set intermediate by finding the five most similar training patients for each test patient based on Euclidean distance. Training data includes patient age, body mass index, diagnosed condition encoded as numeric categories, and six binary indicators A through F along with survival outcomes from complete records. Both training and testing intermediate data undergo standardization to zero mean unit variance ensuring distance calculations are not dominated by features with larger numeric ranges.

Step 16: KNN classifier is configured with k equal to 5 neighbors meaning each prediction is determined by majority vote among the five closest training patients, and Euclidean distance metric for measuring similarity in the standardized feature space. The model is fitted on standardized training features and their corresponding survival outcomes, then generates binary predictions for all testing intermediate observations. The predicted positive rate is 47.45 percent calculated as the proportion of test predictions equal to one, indicating the model expects approximately 47 percent of testing intermediate patients to survive one year based on similarity to training patients.

Step 17: The sixth analysis performs ROC curve analysis by splitting training data into 80 percent training subset and 20 percent validation subset using random state 42 for reproducible partitioning. Complete records with eight numeric features and survival outcome are selected, then split without standardization since logistic regression can handle mixed scale features. Logistic regression is trained on the training subset and predicts survival probabilities on the held out validation subset to simulate performance on unseen data.

Step 18: ROC curve is constructed by computing true positive rate and false positive rate at various probability thresholds from zero to one, creating a curve that shows the tradeoff between sensitivity and specificity across all possible classification thresholds. Area under the ROC curve is calculated as 0.6626 indicating good discrimination ability where the model can distinguish survivors from non survivors better than random guessing. The ROC curve is visualized and saved as a PNG image showing the curved line above the diagonal reference line representing random classifier performance.

Step 19: The seventh analysis examines patient demographic distribution in Testing set advance by grouping observations by smoking status categories after excluding records with missing smoker or age values. For each smoking category zero for non smokers, one for former smokers, and two for current smokers, the analysis counts number of observations and computes mean patient age. The count ratio is calculated as maximum category count divided by minimum category count yielding 4.00 which indicates a fourfold imbalance where the most common smoking status has four times as many patients as the least common status.

Step 20: The eighth analysis computes Pearson correlation coefficient between patient age and body mass index in Testing set intermediate to assess linear relationship strength between these demographic variables. Complete records with both age and BMI values are selected and the correlation is calculated as negative 0.0089 indicating virtually no linear relationship. This near zero correlation suggests age and BMI vary independently in this patient population and do not provide redundant information for survival prediction models.

Step 21: The ninth analysis compares age distributions between training set survivors and testing intermediate population by computing mean ages and their absolute difference. Training survivors are identified as records where Survived 1 year equals one with mean age 53.73 years, while testing intermediate has overall mean age 54.09 years regardless of survival status. The absolute difference of 0.36 years indicates nearly identical age distributions suggesting the testing intermediate population is well matched to training survivors in terms of age demographics.

Step 22: The modeling strategy recommendation synthesizes insights from random forest feature importance analysis and gradient boosting cross validation performance to propose an optimal prediction approach. Random forest identified Patient Body Mass Index as the most important feature with importance score 0.2565 suggesting feature selection should prioritize BMI along with other high importance variables. Gradient boosting achieved mean cross validated AUC of 0.6506 demonstrating ensemble methods effectively combine multiple weak learners for improved prediction accuracy compared to single models.

Step 23: The recommended strategy combines random forest for initial feature importance ranking to identify the most predictive patient characteristics, followed by gradient boosting on the selected features for final survival prediction to maximize accuracy. This two stage approach leverages random forest strength in identifying relevant features through permutation importance while exploiting gradient boosting ability to build powerful ensemble models through sequential error correction. The combination addresses both feature selection to reduce dimensionality and overfitting, and ensemble learning to capture complex nonlinear relationships between patient features and survival outcomes.
