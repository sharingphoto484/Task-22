FLEET OPERATIONS ANALYSIS QC NOTES

PROMPT GOAL

The analysis objective is to optimize fleet utilization reduce maintenance expenses and maximize customer profitability through sophisticated machine learning and statistical methods across three datasets containing customer information driver performance metrics and maintenance records. The project requires implementing ten distinct analytical tasks including Support Vector Machine classification for driver performance Polynomial Regression for maintenance cost modeling K Nearest Neighbors regression for revenue prediction and Exponential Smoothing for cost forecasting. Additional requirements include Gaussian Mixture Model clustering to identify driver performance profiles facility downtime analysis freight type revenue segmentation driver improvement trend analysis maintenance cost ratio calculations and Spearman correlation assessment. The datasets comprise two hundred customer accounts one hundred twenty four unique drivers tracked across thirty six months and two thousand nine hundred twenty maintenance events across one hundred twenty trucks. Machine learning models must be evaluated with specific accuracy metrics coefficient of determination mean squared error and correlation coefficients while generating five publication quality visualizations. The ultimate deliverable provides actionable insights for fleet operations management enabling data driven decisions on driver retention maintenance scheduling customer prioritization and cost optimization strategies.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTIONS

Step 1: The first task performs Support Vector Machine classification on driver monthly metrics by aggregating performance indicators across all months for each of the one hundred twenty four drivers computing mean on time delivery rate mean average miles per gallon total trips completed and total miles driven. A binary target variable is created where drivers with mean on time delivery rate greater than or equal to zero point five are classified as high performers encoded as one otherwise zero though the actual data shows only one driver meets this threshold creating extreme class imbalance. The aggregated driver data is split into seventy percent training and thirty percent testing sets using random seed twenty five without stratification due to insufficient samples in the minority class then an SVM model with radial basis function kernel is trained and evaluated achieving perfect accuracy of one point zero on the test set.

Step 2: The visualization for SVM classification generates a two dimensional scatter plot with mean on time delivery rate on the horizontal axis and mean average miles per gallon on the vertical axis where each point represents an individual driver colored by the predicted class label from the trained model. The coolwarm color scheme differentiates between predicted low performers shown in blue and high performers shown in red with color bar legend providing clear interpretation. This spatial representation reveals how the SVM decision boundary separates drivers based on their efficiency and reliability metrics enabling visual identification of performance patterns.

Step 3: The second task implements Polynomial Regression on maintenance records by filtering only preventive maintenance type events and constructing degree three polynomial features using odometer reading as the independent variable and total cost as the dependent variable. The preventive maintenance dataset is split into eighty five percent training and fifteen percent testing portions with random seed thirty ensuring reproducible results while the polynomial transformation creates quadratic and cubic terms to capture nonlinear relationships between vehicle mileage and maintenance expenses. Model evaluation yields a coefficient of determination of negative zero point zero two two two on the test set indicating poor predictive performance where the model performs worse than a simple mean baseline suggesting preventive maintenance costs may not follow a consistent polynomial pattern with odometer readings.

Step 4: The polynomial regression visualization displays actual testing set observations as blue scatter points with odometer reading in miles on the horizontal axis and total cost in dollars on the vertical axis overlaid with a red fitted curve representing the degree three polynomial regression prediction across the full range of odometer values. This plot reveals the nonlinear relationship captured by the cubic model though the negative R squared suggests high variance in actual costs relative to the fitted trend potentially due to varying maintenance complexity parts availability or facility specific pricing differences. The visualization enables stakeholders to assess whether odometer based cost predictions are reliable for budgeting preventive maintenance expenses.

Step 5: The third task combines driver monthly metrics and customer data for K Nearest Neighbors regression by first aggregating driver revenue by extracting year month from the month column and summing total revenue across all drivers per month creating thirty six monthly aggregate observations. Customer features are prepared by calculating monthly contribution as annual revenue potential divided by twelve and creating binary indicator variables for customer type equals Dedicated and account status equals Active then cross joining customers with months to generate monthly customer records. The merged dataset uses credit terms days and the two binary indicators as predictor features with monthly total driver revenue as the target variable split into seventy five percent training and twenty five percent testing with random seed forty and a KNN regressor with seven neighbors is trained achieving a mean squared error of fifty eight billion seven hundred two million on the test set.

Step 6: The KNN regression visualization creates an actual versus predicted scatter plot where the horizontal axis shows actual monthly revenue values from the test set and the vertical axis displays corresponding predicted values from the K Nearest Neighbors model with points colored green and a red dashed diagonal reference line indicating perfect predictions. The scatter pattern reveals prediction accuracy where points closer to the diagonal line indicate better model performance while deviations suggest prediction errors with the extremely high mean squared error indicating substantial variability in revenue predictions. This diagnostic plot helps identify whether the model systematically over predicts or under predicts revenue and whether prediction errors are homoscedastic across the revenue range.

Step 7: The fourth task applies Simple Exponential Smoothing to monthly maintenance costs by aggregating total cost from all maintenance events grouping by year month extracted from maintenance date and sorting chronologically to create a time series spanning January two thousand twenty two through December two thousand twenty four with thirty six monthly observations. The exponential smoothing model is fitted with smoothing parameter alpha equals zero point three which gives thirty percent weight to the most recent observation and seventy percent to the previous smoothed value creating a balance between responsiveness and stability. After model fitting six month ahead forecasts are generated beyond December two thousand twenty four with the sixth month forecast predicting one hundred fifty six thousand one hundred thirty four dollars and fifty cents in monthly maintenance expenditure.

Step 8: The exponential smoothing visualization generates a line plot spanning January two thousand twenty two through June two thousand twenty five with month on the horizontal axis and monthly maintenance cost in dollars on the vertical axis displaying historical actual values as a solid blue line with circular markers and the six month forecast as an orange dashed line with square markers. This temporal visualization enables operations managers to observe historical cost patterns identify seasonal trends or unusual spikes and evaluate the reasonableness of future projections for budget planning purposes. The smoothing approach provides conservative predictions useful for establishing maintenance reserve funds though the constant alpha parameter assumes stationary cost patterns that may not hold if fleet composition or maintenance policies change.

Step 9: The fifth task implements Gaussian Mixture Model clustering on driver performance by aggregating mean average miles per gallon mean on time delivery rate and mean average idle hours across all months for each driver then standardizing these three features to zero mean and unit variance ensuring equal weighting in the clustering algorithm. A GMM with exactly four components using full covariance type and random seed fifteen is fitted to the standardized features allowing each cluster to have its own ellipsoidal shape and orientation in the three dimensional feature space capturing complex multivariate patterns. After fitting cluster assignments are predicted for all drivers and cluster three is identified as having the highest average on time delivery rate of zero point four six two three suggesting this group represents the most reliable driver performance profile.

Step 10: The GMM clustering visualization creates a three dimensional scatter plot with mean average miles per gallon on the horizontal axis mean on time delivery rate on the depth axis and mean average idle hours on the vertical axis where each point represents a driver colored by their assigned cluster from zero to three using the viridis color scheme. This multidimensional representation reveals natural groupings in driver behavior where cluster separation indicates distinct performance profiles such as fuel efficient low idle drivers versus less efficient high idle drivers enabling targeted training interventions or incentive programs. The full covariance model allows clusters to capture correlations between metrics such as drivers who achieve high fuel efficiency while maintaining low idle times representing optimal operational behavior.

Step 11: The sixth task identifies the facility location with maximum average downtime per maintenance event by grouping all maintenance records by facility location field and computing arithmetic mean of downtime hours across all events at each facility. Among all facilities Los Angeles is identified as having the highest mean downtime of twenty six point five nine hours indicating operational inefficiencies such as parts availability issues inadequate staffing complex repair specialization or facility capacity constraints. This metric provides actionable intelligence for operations managers to investigate root causes of extended downtime at Los Angeles and implement process improvements such as improved inventory management additional technician training or workflow optimization.

Step 12: The seventh task performs customer segmentation by grouping all customer records by primary freight type and summing annual revenue potential across all customers within each freight category to identify the most valuable market segment. Food and Beverage freight type is identified as generating the maximum total revenue potential of one hundred eight million seven hundred eighty seven thousand seven hundred sixty six dollars representing the largest opportunity for fleet revenue optimization. This insight enables strategic resource allocation such as prioritizing Food and Beverage customer relationships investing in specialized refrigerated equipment or tailoring service offerings to capture greater market share in this high value segment.

Step 13: The eighth task analyzes driver improvement trends by sorting monthly records chronologically for each driver and computing linear regression slope for on time delivery rate using sequential month indices as the independent variable and delivery rate as the dependent variable where positive slopes indicate performance improvement over time. Driver DRV00127 is identified as having the maximum positive slope of zero point zero zero five two three eight indicating the greatest improvement trajectory in on time delivery performance across the analysis period. This finding suggests DRV00127 has successfully adapted to operational requirements potentially through experience gained route familiarity improved time management or effective response to coaching making this driver a candidate for recognition or a case study for best practices.

Step 14: The ninth task calculates maintenance cost ratios by filtering records where maintenance type equals Repair representing emergency unplanned maintenance and summing total cost to nine hundred forty five thousand three hundred seventy five dollars and forty four cents then filtering for Preventive maintenance summing to nine hundred sixty three thousand five hundred seventy six dollars and thirty four cents. The ratio of emergency to scheduled maintenance costs is zero point nine eight one one indicating that emergency repairs cost nearly as much as preventive maintenance suggesting effective preventive maintenance programs that minimize costly breakdowns though opportunities may exist to further reduce emergency repairs through predictive maintenance technologies. This balanced ratio indicates reasonable fleet health though the ideal target would be a lower ratio where preventive maintenance significantly outweighs emergency repairs.

Step 15: The tenth task assesses the relationship between fuel efficiency and idle behavior by computing Spearman rank correlation coefficient between average miles per gallon and average idle hours across all driver month observations using the nonparametric Spearman method to capture potential nonlinear monotonic relationships. The analysis yields a correlation of negative zero point zero one two one with p value zero point four one nine indicating a very weak negative correlation that is not statistically significant suggesting idle hours are not a strong predictor of fuel efficiency degradation in this fleet. This finding is somewhat counterintuitive as conventional wisdom suggests excessive idling should reduce fuel efficiency but may reflect that other factors such as driving speed route characteristics vehicle condition or load weight have stronger influences on miles per gallon performance in this operational context.
