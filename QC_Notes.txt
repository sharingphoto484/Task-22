QUALITY CONTROL NOTES FOR WELLBEING PATTERNS ANALYSIS

PROMPT GOAL

This analysis conducts a formal quantitative assessment of global wellbeing patterns using three datasets from 2015, 2017, and 2019. The primary objective is to standardize heterogeneous datasets by harmonizing column names and retaining only countries present across all three years to ensure longitudinal consistency. The analysis employs multiple statistical techniques including standardized multiple linear regression, correlation analysis, coefficient of variation assessment, and principal component analysis to understand the determinants of wellbeing scores. Additionally, the assessment evaluates temporal trends through year over year percentage changes and visualizes the relationship between GDP and wellbeing scores across the three time periods. All preprocessing steps require removing missing or nonnumeric values to maintain data integrity before any statistical modeling or analysis is performed. The final deliverable includes specific quantitative metrics rounded to precise decimal places as specified in the requirements along with a comprehensive visualization of GDP score relationships.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTION

Step 1: The analysis begins by loading three CSV files corresponding to years 2015, 2017, and 2019, each containing country level wellbeing data with varying column naming conventions. Each dataset uses different terminology for similar concepts such as Economy versus GDP per Capita or Family versus Social Support, requiring careful mapping during the import process. The initial load confirms that 2015 has 158 countries, 2017 has 155 countries, and 2019 has 156 countries before any preprocessing steps are applied.

Step 2: Column standardization is performed by renaming all variables to a consistent schema across the three datasets with unified names including Country, Score, GDP, SocialSupport, Health, Freedom, Generosity, and Corruption. This harmonization process maps Trust Government Corruption to Corruption in 2015, Economy GDP per Capita to GDP in multiple years, and Family to SocialSupport ensuring semantic consistency. The standardized naming convention enables seamless merging and comparison of data across different years without ambiguity in variable definitions.

Step 3: Data cleaning is executed by converting all numeric columns to proper numeric data types and coercing any non numeric values to missing values using pandas to numeric function with error coercion. After conversion, all rows containing any missing values in the eight key variables are completely removed from each dataset to maintain analytical rigor. This cleaning process ensures that every retained observation has complete information across all wellbeing determinants required for subsequent statistical modeling.

Step 4: Country intersection analysis identifies which nations appear in all three datasets by computing the set intersection of country names across 2015, 2017, and 2019. The analysis reveals that 144 countries are present in all three years, which becomes the final analytical sample to ensure longitudinal comparability. Only these 144 common countries are retained in each yearly dataset, filtering out any countries that have data for only one or two of the three time periods.

Step 5: The three yearly datasets containing only common countries are concatenated into a single merged dataset with 432 total observations representing 144 countries measured across three time points. Each row in the merged dataset includes a Year identifier allowing for both cross sectional analysis within specific years and longitudinal analysis across time periods. This merged structure supports analyses that require all years combined such as the principal component analysis while maintaining the ability to subset by year for year specific analyses.

Step 6: Multiple linear regression modeling is performed exclusively on the 2019 subset using Score as the dependent variable and six predictors including GDP, SocialSupport, Health, Freedom, Generosity, and Corruption. Before model estimation, all six predictor variables are standardized to have mean zero and standard deviation one using sklearn StandardScaler to enable direct comparison of coefficient magnitudes. The fitted model achieves an R squared value of 0.777 indicating that the six standardized predictors collectively explain approximately 77.7 percent of the variance in wellbeing scores for 2019.

Step 7: Pearson correlation coefficients between GDP and Score are calculated separately for each of the three years 2015, 2017, and 2019 using scipy pearsonr function. The correlations are 0.7832 for 2015, 0.8291 for 2017, and 0.7987 for 2019, showing consistently strong positive associations between economic prosperity and wellbeing across all time periods. The simple arithmetic mean of these three correlations yields an average correlation of 0.8037 rounded to four decimal places as required by the specifications.

Step 8: Coefficient of variation is computed for the Score variable in each year by dividing the standard deviation by the mean, providing a standardized measure of relative variability. The CVs are 0.2167 for 2015, 0.2128 for 2017, and 0.2045 for 2019, indicating slight decreases in relative score dispersion over time. Taking the arithmetic mean of these three coefficients produces a mean CV of 0.2114 rounded to four decimals, representing the average relative variability in wellbeing scores across the study period.

Step 9: Principal component analysis is conducted on all six predictor variables using the complete merged dataset with all 432 observations after standardizing the predictors across all years combined. The PCA decomposition reveals that the first principal component explains 0.474 or 47.4 percent of the total variance in the six dimensional predictor space. This proportion indicates that nearly half of the variability in wellbeing determinants can be captured by a single underlying dimension, suggesting moderate dimensionality in the predictor structure.

Step 10: Year over year percentage changes in Score are calculated for each country across two transition periods from 2015 to 2017 and from 2017 to 2019 using the formula new minus old divided by old times 100. For each country, the percentage changes from both periods are averaged together to produce a country specific mean change rate. The overall mean across all 144 countries of these averaged percentage changes is 0.987 percent rounded to three decimals, indicating a modest positive trend in wellbeing scores over the study period.

Step 11: The GDP Score Trend Lines Chart is constructed with GDP on the x axis and Score on the y axis, displaying data points and fitted regression lines for all three years simultaneously. Separate simple linear regression models are fit for each year relating GDP to Score, producing distinct slopes and intercepts for 2015, 2017, and 2019. Each year is visualized with a unique color including blue for 2015, green for 2017, and red for 2019 to facilitate visual comparison of temporal trends in the GDP wellbeing relationship.

Step 12: The slope coefficients from the three year specific simple linear regressions are extracted with particular focus on the 2019 regression line slope. The 2019 slope is 2.2756 rounded to four decimal places, indicating that a one unit increase in GDP is associated with an approximately 2.28 point increase in wellbeing Score within the 2019 data. This slope quantifies the strength of the linear GDP Score relationship in the most recent year of observation.

Step 13: From the standardized multiple regression model fitted on 2019 data, the absolute values of all six standardized coefficients are compared to identify the most influential predictor. GDP emerges as the predictor with the highest absolute standardized coefficient at 0.3326, exceeding the absolute coefficients of SocialSupport, Health, Freedom, Generosity, and Corruption. This result indicates that when all predictors are measured on the same standardized scale, GDP has the strongest unique association with wellbeing scores after controlling for the other five determinants.

Step 14: All key outputs are systematically reported with appropriate rounding specifications including R squared to three decimals at 0.777, average correlation to four decimals at 0.8037, mean coefficient of variation to four decimals at 0.2114, PC1 variance to three decimals at 0.474, mean YoY change to three decimals at 0.987, and 2019 slope to four decimals at 2.2756. The top predictor is identified as GDP based on the highest absolute standardized regression coefficient. The visualization file GDP Score Trend Lines Chart is saved as a PNG image showing the three overlaid regression relationships across time periods.

Step 15: The complete analysis pipeline ensures methodological rigor through standardization of column names, retention of only common countries, removal of missing values, and appropriate statistical preprocessing before modeling. All statistical computations are executed using established libraries including pandas for data manipulation, numpy for numerical operations, scipy for correlation analysis, and sklearn for regression and PCA. The workflow produces reproducible quantitative results that directly address each component of the formal assessment requirements while maintaining data integrity throughout all transformation and analysis steps.
