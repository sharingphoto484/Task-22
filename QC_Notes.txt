PROMPT GOAL

This analysis aims to conduct comprehensive statistical modeling and machine learning on three transportation datasets spanning 2017 containing traffic volume measurements, climate observations, and detailed weather metrics. The primary objective is to quantify relationships between atmospheric conditions and vehicular flow through multiple linear regression, segment traffic patterns using unsupervised clustering, and reduce meteorological variable dimensionality using principal component analysis. Advanced time series forecasting using ARIMA models predicts future traffic volumes while Granger causality tests assess whether air quality metrics can predict traffic flow patterns. The analysis incorporates hierarchical clustering for climate profiling, random forest regression for nonlinear traffic prediction, logistic regression for holiday classification, and correlation analysis between visibility and traffic density. Statistical hypothesis testing using Kolmogorov Smirnov validates distribution assumptions while peak hour identification supports resource allocation planning. All analyses generate corresponding visualizations and report key statistical metrics without producing summary files.


STEP BY STEP SOLUTION

Step 1: The analysis begins by loading three Excel datasets containing traffic volume with timestamp and weather event information, climate data with daily temperature humidity wind and pressure metrics, and comprehensive weather observations with pollution visibility and precipitation measurements. Each dataset is read using pandas Excel reading functionality and initial data shape verification confirms successful loading with traffic data containing 8736 hourly observations, climate data containing 367 daily records, and weather data containing 6505 meteorological observations. This foundational step ensures all required data sources are accessible for subsequent analytical processing.

Step 2: For multiple linear regression analysis, daily traffic volumes are created by extracting dates from hourly timestamps and aggregating traffic volume through summation for each unique date. The climate dataset undergoes date standardization to ensure consistent date formatting between both datasets enabling proper merge operations. Daily traffic totals are then merged with climate observations on matching dates creating a unified dataset containing daily traffic volume alongside meantemp humidity wind speed and meanpressure variables for regression modeling.

Step 3: Multiple linear regression model construction uses four climate variables as independent predictors including mean temperature, humidity, wind speed, and mean pressure to explain variation in daily total traffic volume as the dependent variable. The regression model fits on the complete merged dataset and extracts the coefficient of determination quantifying the proportion of traffic volume variance explained by atmospheric conditions. The resulting R squared value of 0.066377 indicates that climate variables explain approximately 6.64 percent of daily traffic volume variation suggesting other factors beyond basic weather conditions significantly influence traffic patterns.

Step 4: A scatter plot visualization displays the relationship between mean temperature and daily traffic volume with temperature on the horizontal axis and traffic volume on the vertical axis. To overlay a regression line, predictions are generated across the temperature range while holding humidity wind speed and pressure at their mean values demonstrating the partial relationship between temperature and traffic. The plot is saved as a high resolution PNG file providing visual confirmation of the weak positive relationship between temperature and traffic volume consistent with the low R squared value.

Step 5: K means clustering analysis requires feature engineering to extract hour of day as integer values ranging from 0 representing midnight to 23 representing 11 PM from the timestamp column. Traffic volume normalization divides each observation by the dataset maximum creating normalized values between 0 and 1 ensuring equal scaling between hour and volume features. These two features form the input matrix for k means clustering enabling pattern discovery based on temporal position and traffic intensity.

Step 6: The k means algorithm with exactly five clusters applies iterative partitioning to group observations with similar hour and normalized volume characteristics using random seed 42 for reproducibility. After convergence, cluster assignments are added to the traffic dataframe and average traffic volume is calculated for each cluster to identify behavioral patterns. Cluster 3 emerges with the highest average traffic volume of 1510.90 indicating this cluster captures peak traffic periods with elevated vehicular flow.

Step 7: K means clustering visualization creates a scatter plot with hour of day on the horizontal axis ranging 0 to 23 and normalized traffic volume on the vertical axis ranging 0 to 1. Each of the five clusters receives distinct color coding including red blue green orange and purple allowing visual differentiation of temporal traffic patterns. The plot reveals cluster separation based on time of day and traffic intensity with cluster 3 visually concentrated in mid day high traffic periods while other clusters represent off peak early morning late evening and moderate traffic patterns.

Step 8: Principal component analysis begins with feature selection choosing seven meteorological variables including air pollution index, humidity, wind speed, visibility in miles, dew point, temperature, and clouds all from the weather dataset. Missing values are removed to ensure complete case analysis and all features undergo standardization by subtracting mean and dividing by standard deviation achieving zero mean and unit variance. Standardization ensures variables on different scales contribute equally to principal component extraction preventing dominance by variables with larger absolute magnitudes.

Step 9: PCA algorithm applies orthogonal transformation to the standardized feature matrix extracting principal components that capture maximum variance in descending order. The explained variance ratio for each component quantifies the proportion of total variance explained enabling assessment of dimensionality reduction effectiveness. Cumulative variance calculation sums the first three components yielding 0.626954 indicating approximately 62.70 percent of meteorological variation can be represented using just three principal components instead of the original seven variables.

Step 10: The PCA biplot visualization displays principal component scores as scattered points with PC1 on the horizontal axis explaining 28.59 percent variance and PC2 on the vertical axis explaining 19.58 percent variance. Loading vectors appear as arrows emanating from the origin with direction and length indicating how original variables contribute to principal components. Feature labels positioned at arrow endpoints show that temperature dew point and air pollution index strongly influence PC1 while humidity and clouds all contribute more substantially to PC2 revealing underlying meteorological structure.

Step 11: ARIMA time series forecasting starts by aggregating hourly traffic observations into daily totals through date extraction and grouping then sorting chronologically to create a proper time series sequence. The ARIMA model with autoregressive order 2, differencing order 1, and moving average order 2 fits to the complete daily traffic time series capturing trend seasonality and autocorrelation patterns. After model estimation, seven step ahead forecasts generate predictions for days beyond the final observed date with the seventh day forecast producing 29510.36 as the predicted daily traffic volume.

Step 12: ARIMA forecast visualization creates a line plot with dates on the horizontal axis spanning the entire 2017 period and daily traffic volume on the vertical axis. Historical actual values plot as a solid line showing observed traffic patterns throughout the year while the seven day forecast extends beyond the final date as a red dashed line with marker points. This visualization demonstrates forecast continuity with historical patterns and provides transportation planners with anticipated traffic volumes for short term capacity planning.

Step 13: Granger causality testing requires constructing two aligned time series for daily air pollution index and daily traffic volume over the overlapping May through December 2017 period. Traffic data undergoes date extraction and daily summation while weather data undergoes date extraction and air pollution index averaging to create daily pollution means. Both series merge on matching dates and sort chronologically forming a bivariate time series suitable for Granger causality hypothesis testing.

Step 14: The Granger causality test with lag order 4 assesses whether past values of daily air pollution index provide statistically significant information for predicting current daily traffic volume beyond what traffic's own past values provide. The test produces an F statistic and corresponding p value of 0.067078 at the fourth lag indicating marginal statistical significance at conventional alpha 0.05 threshold. This borderline result suggests weak evidence that air pollution Granger causes traffic volume with the relationship not definitively established at strict significance levels.

Step 15: Dual time series visualization normalizes both air pollution and traffic volume to 0 to 1 scale by subtracting minimum and dividing by range enabling comparative plotting on the same vertical axis. The plot displays May through December 2017 dates on the horizontal axis with blue line representing normalized pollution and red line representing normalized traffic. Visual inspection reveals some temporal co movement between series supporting the marginal Granger causality result while also showing substantial independent variation.

Step 16: Hierarchical clustering on climate data selects four features including meantemp humidity wind speed and meanpressure representing comprehensive daily atmospheric conditions. Standardization transforms these features to zero mean and unit variance ensuring equal contribution to distance calculations in the clustering algorithm. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering with Ward linkage method and Euclidean distance metric builds a dendrogram by iteratively merging the most similar observations or clusters until all observations unite in a single hierarchy.

Step 17: The dendrogram is cut to produce exactly six clusters and cluster assignments are added to the climate dataframe enabling within cluster statistical analysis. Average mean pressure calculation for each cluster identifies which cluster contains days with highest atmospheric pressure conditions. Cluster 5 exhibits dramatically higher average mean pressure of 7679.33 compared to other clusters ranging from 980 to 1016 indicating this cluster likely contains data quality issues or measurement errors given physically implausible pressure values.

Step 18: Random forest regression requires temporal feature engineering extracting hour as integer 0 through 23, day of week as integer 0 through 6 representing Monday through Sunday, and binary events indicator encoding True as 1 and False as 0. The dataset splits into 80 percent training and 20 percent testing sets using random seed 42 for reproducibility while maintaining representative samples in both partitions. Random forest with 100 trees trains on the training set learning complex nonlinear relationships between temporal features and traffic volume without explicit functional form specification.

Step 19: Model evaluation computes predictions on the held out test set and calculates mean absolute error measuring average absolute deviation between predicted and actual traffic volumes. The resulting MAE of 287.67 vehicles indicates typical prediction error magnitude providing interpretable model performance assessment. Feature importance extraction from the trained random forest reveals hour of day contributes most substantially to predictions followed by events indicator and day of week showing traffic volume depends primarily on time of day with events providing secondary predictive value.

Step 20: Logistic regression classification creates binary holiday target encoding non null holiday names as 1 and null values as 0 then selects five weather features including temperature humidity wind speed air pollution and clouds. The dataset splits 75 percent training and 25 percent testing with stratified sampling maintaining class balance proportions in both sets using random seed 42. Logistic regression trains on weather features to predict holiday occurrence and achieves 0.998771 classification accuracy on test set indicating weather conditions provide almost perfect holiday discrimination likely because holidays occur on specific calendar dates with coincidentally distinct weather rather than causal weather holiday relationships.

Step 21: Pearson correlation analysis between visibility and traffic requires date level aggregation computing mean traffic volume per date from traffic data and mean visibility per date from weather data over the overlapping May through December period. The merged dataset pairs daily mean visibility with daily mean traffic volume enabling correlation coefficient calculation. The resulting Pearson correlation of 0.070918 with p value 0.287362 indicates weak positive linear association that lacks statistical significance suggesting visibility and traffic volume are largely independent variables during this period.

Step 22: Kolmogorov Smirnov goodness of fit test evaluates whether daily humidity values from climate data follow a normal distribution by comparing empirical humidity distribution against theoretical normal distribution. The test estimates normal distribution parameters including mean and standard deviation from the humidity sample then computes maximum absolute difference between empirical and theoretical cumulative distributions. The resulting p value of 0.239907 exceeds conventional 0.05 significance threshold indicating insufficient evidence to reject normality hypothesis suggesting humidity values are consistent with normal distribution.

Step 23: Peak traffic hour identification extracts hour of day from all timestamps and groups observations by hour computing total traffic volume summed across all dates for each of the 24 hours. Comparison across hourly totals identifies hour 12 corresponding to noon as having maximum cumulative traffic volume of 584523 vehicles across the entire year. This finding supports resource allocation decisions by identifying mid day as the period requiring maximum road capacity and traffic management resources for urban transportation planning.
