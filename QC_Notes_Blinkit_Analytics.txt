QC NOTES FOR BLINKIT RETAIL ANALYTICS PROJECT

PROMPT GOAL:

The project aims to perform comprehensive retail analytics on Blinkit order transaction data, product inventory specifications, and marketing campaign performance metrics using advanced machine learning and statistical techniques. The analysis focuses on demand forecasting using time series models, regression techniques for order value and price prediction, and classification methods for delivery success prediction to optimize inventory management. Marketing effectiveness is evaluated through Granger causality testing and partial least squares regression to understand the relationship between marketing spend and revenue generation. Dimensionality reduction techniques including principal component analysis and feature importance analysis identify key drivers of pricing and customer behavior. Bootstrap confidence intervals and variance analysis provide robust statistical inference for margin estimation and marketing channel performance consistency evaluation.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTIONS:

Step 1: The first step involves loading three Excel datasets containing orders with 5000 records, products with 268 records, and marketing campaigns with 5400 records into pandas dataframes. All required Python libraries are imported including statsmodels for time series analysis, scikit learn for machine learning models, and visualization libraries for generating charts. The data loading ensures all columns are properly recognized including order dates, delivery times, product specifications, and marketing metrics.

Step 2: For ARIMA time series forecasting, daily revenue is aggregated from the marketing performance dataset by grouping all campaigns by date and summing the revenue generated field. The aggregated time series is sorted chronologically to create a sequential dataset spanning 600 days of marketing activity. This daily revenue series becomes the foundation for predicting future revenue patterns using autoregressive integrated moving average methodology.

Step 3: The revenue time series is split into training and testing sets using an 80 20 ratio where the first 480 days are used for model training and the remaining 120 days are held out for validation. This temporal split ensures that the model learns from historical patterns and is evaluated on future unseen data. The split preserves the time order which is critical for time series analysis unlike random splitting used in cross sectional data.

Step 4: An ARIMA model with order parameters p equals 2, d equals 1, and q equals 2 is fitted on the training revenue data. The p parameter of 2 means the model uses two previous time points for autoregression, d equals 1 applies first order differencing to achieve stationarity, and q equals 2 incorporates two lagged forecast errors in the moving average component. These parameters balance model complexity with predictive accuracy for daily revenue patterns.

Step 5: One step ahead forecasts are generated for each day in the testing period by predicting the next day revenue value iteratively. The mean absolute error is calculated by taking the average of absolute differences between actual and forecasted revenues across all 120 test days. The resulting MAE of 5282.67 dollars indicates the average prediction error magnitude which helps assess forecast reliability for revenue planning.

Step 6: A line plot visualization is created showing the training data in blue, actual test revenue in green, and forecasted revenue in red dashed line across the date axis. This visualization allows stakeholders to visually assess how well the ARIMA model captures revenue trends and seasonal patterns. The plot is saved as arima forecast png for documentation and presentation purposes.

Step 7: For k nearest neighbors regression, customer level features are engineered by calculating average order total and order count for each customer from the orders dataset. These aggregated metrics capture customer purchasing patterns and frequency which serve as proxies for product preferences and category diversity. A normalized total feature is created by dividing individual order totals by the mean order total to standardize the scale.

Step 8: The KNN features including average order total, order count, and normalized total are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using StandardScaler. Standardization is essential for KNN because the algorithm uses Euclidean distance which is sensitive to feature scales. Without standardization, features with larger numeric ranges would dominate the distance calculations.

Step 9: The dataset is split into 80 percent training and 20 percent testing sets, then a KNN regressor with k equals 7 neighbors is fitted on the scaled training data. The choice of 7 neighbors balances between overfitting with too few neighbors and oversmoothing with too many neighbors. The Euclidean distance metric measures similarity between customer profiles to find the nearest neighbors.

Step 10: Predictions are generated for the test set and R squared coefficient of determination is calculated yielding 0.9969 which indicates that 99.69 percent of variance in order totals is explained by the customer features. This extremely high R squared suggests strong predictive relationships between customer ordering patterns and individual transaction values. A scatter plot comparing actual versus predicted order totals with a reference diagonal line visualizes prediction accuracy.

Step 11: Decision tree regression is applied to predict product prices using four features from the products dataset including margin percentage, shelf life days, minimum stock level, and maximum stock level. The decision tree is configured with maximum depth of 6 to prevent overfitting and minimum samples split of 10 to ensure sufficient data at each node. Random state 42 ensures reproducibility of the tree structure across multiple runs.

Step 12: After training the decision tree, feature importances are extracted which measure how much each feature contributes to reducing prediction error based on weighted impurity decrease. The feature importances are normalized to sum to 1.0 making them interpretable as relative importance percentages. The analysis identifies max stock level as the most important feature indicating that inventory capacity is the strongest price determinant.

Step 13: A horizontal bar chart displays all four features sorted by their importance scores with max stock level showing the highest value. This visualization helps product managers understand which factors most influence pricing decisions. The chart is saved as feature importance png for reporting to stakeholders.

Step 14: For Naive Bayes classification, a binary target variable on time is created where 1 indicates the delivery status equals On Time and 0 represents late deliveries. The promised hour is extracted from the promised delivery time timestamp to capture time of day effects on delivery performance. Customer order frequency is calculated by counting how many orders each customer has placed which may correlate with delivery address familiarity.

Step 15: Gaussian Naive Bayes classifier is trained assuming that the three features including promised hour, order total, and customer order frequency follow normal distributions within each class. The Naive Bayes assumption treats features as conditionally independent given the class label which simplifies probability calculations. Despite this simplifying assumption, the model achieves 70.10 percent classification accuracy on predicting on time delivery.

Step 16: A confusion matrix heatmap is generated showing true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives for the delivery prediction task. The heatmap uses color intensity to represent the count of predictions in each category making misclassification patterns visually apparent. This helps identify whether the model tends to over predict or under predict on time deliveries.

Step 17: Granger causality testing examines whether daily marketing spend helps predict daily revenue by aggregating spend and revenue by date. The test uses lag order 5 meaning it checks if spend from up to 5 days prior provides statistically significant information about current revenue. The F statistic of 0.68 from the statistical test compares restricted models without spend lags versus unrestricted models with spend lags.

Step 18: Principal component analysis reduces the five product features including price, margin percentage, shelf life, minimum stock level, and maximum stock level into three uncorrelated principal components. The first principal component captures 23.39 percent of total variance representing the dominant pattern of variation across product characteristics. These principal component scores are then used as predictors in linear regression to predict maximum retail price.

Step 19: Partial least squares regression creates two latent components from four marketing metrics including impressions, clicks, conversions, and ROAS to predict order totals. The PLS method finds linear combinations of marketing features that maximize covariance with the order total outcome. The impressions coefficient of negative 0.0042 indicates a small inverse relationship between impression volume and individual order values in the fitted model.

Step 20: Bootstrap confidence interval estimation draws 1000 random samples with replacement from the observed margin percentages to create a sampling distribution of the mean. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these 1000 bootstrap means form the 95 percent confidence interval bounds. The confidence interval width of 1.81 percentage points quantifies the uncertainty in estimating the true population mean margin percentage providing a range for business planning.
