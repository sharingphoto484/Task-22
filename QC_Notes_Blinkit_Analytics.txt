QC NOTES FOR BLINKIT RETAIL ANALYTICS PROJECT

PROMPT GOAL:

The project performs comprehensive retail analytics on Blinkit datasets including 5000 order transactions, 268 product records, and 5400 marketing campaign records using advanced machine learning and statistical techniques to optimize inventory management and marketing return on investment. Time series forecasting with ARIMA predicts daily revenue patterns, while regression methods including k nearest neighbors, decision trees, and partial least squares model order values and product pricing relationships. Classification algorithms including Naive Bayes predict delivery success probability, and dimensionality reduction techniques using principal component analysis identify key pricing drivers. Statistical testing methods including Granger causality examine marketing spend effectiveness, bootstrap methods quantify confidence intervals for margin estimation, and variance analysis evaluates channel performance consistency. The analysis integrates demand forecasting, customer behavior modeling, delivery performance prediction, and marketing optimization to provide actionable insights for retail operations and strategic decision making.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTIONS:

Step 1: The analysis begins by loading three Excel files into pandas dataframes including blinkit orders with order transactions and delivery information, blinkit products with pricing and inventory specifications, and blinkit marketing performance with campaign metrics and revenue data. All required Python libraries are imported including statsmodels for time series analysis, scikit learn for machine learning algorithms, numpy for numerical computations, and matplotlib with seaborn for visualization generation. The data loading process validates that all columns are properly recognized including datetime fields for order and delivery timestamps, numeric fields for financial metrics, and categorical fields for status and channel classifications.

Step 2: For ARIMA time series forecasting, the marketing performance dataset is aggregated by date to calculate total daily revenue by grouping all campaign records by their date field and summing the revenue generated values. The resulting daily revenue series is sorted in chronological order to maintain temporal sequence which is essential for time series modeling. This aggregated daily revenue becomes the target variable for forecasting future revenue patterns using autoregressive integrated moving average methodology.

Step 3: The daily revenue time series is split into training and testing sets using a specified percentage ratio where the earlier portion is designated for model training and the later portion is held out for validation. This temporal split preserves the chronological ordering of observations ensuring that the model learns from historical data and is evaluated on future unseen data. The split methodology differs from random cross validation because time series analysis requires respecting temporal dependencies and avoiding data leakage from future to past.

Step 4: An ARIMA model is configured with specified order parameters for autoregressive terms, first order differencing to achieve stationarity, and moving average components. The autoregressive parameter means the model uses revenue values from previous time points to predict the next value, capturing short term autocorrelation patterns. The differencing parameter applies differencing to remove trend and make the series stationary, while the moving average parameter incorporates lagged forecast errors to model the moving average structure.

Step 5: The fitted ARIMA model generates one step ahead forecasts for each day in the test period by iteratively predicting the next day revenue value. Mean absolute error is calculated as the average of absolute differences between actual and forecasted revenues across all test days, quantifying the typical prediction error magnitude. This metric provides a measure for assessing forecast reliability and model performance on unseen data.

Step 6: A line plot visualization is generated with date on the x axis and daily revenue on the y axis, displaying three series including training data, actual test revenue, and forecasted revenue as a dashed line. This visualization enables stakeholders to visually assess how well the ARIMA model captures revenue trends, seasonal patterns, and overall trajectory compared to observed values. The plot is saved as a PNG file for documentation and reporting purposes.

Step 7: The prompt requires creating three specific KNN features including average product price per customer, count of distinct categories ordered, and average margin percentage which necessitate linking orders to products via product identifier, but the orders dataset lacks this linking column making the specified analysis impossible. As a methodological workaround, the implementation creates alternative proxy features from the orders dataset alone including average order total per customer calculated by grouping orders by customer identifier and computing mean order values, customer order count representing purchase frequency, and normalized order total created by dividing each order total by the overall mean. This proxy approach deviates from the prompt specification by using customer aggregated order metrics rather than product based features, fundamentally changing the analysis from product portfolio characteristics to customer spending behavior patterns.

Step 8: The KNN features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using the StandardScaler transformer from scikit learn. Standardization is critical for k nearest neighbors because the algorithm computes Euclidean distances between observations and features with larger numeric ranges would disproportionately influence distance calculations. After standardization all features contribute equally to the similarity metric regardless of their original measurement scales.

Step 9: The standardized feature matrix and order total target variable are split into training and testing sets using random sampling to create independent validation data. A k nearest neighbors regressor is instantiated with a specified number of neighbors and Euclidean distance metric, then fitted on the scaled training data. The choice of neighbor count balances between using too few neighbors which causes overfitting to noise and using too many neighbors which oversmooths patterns and reduces model flexibility.

Step 10: The trained KNN model generates predictions for the test set by finding the nearest training observations to each test point and averaging their order total values. The R squared coefficient of determination is calculated comparing predicted versus actual order totals to quantify the proportion of variance explained by the model. A scatter plot is created with actual order totals on the x axis and predicted order totals on the y axis along with a diagonal reference line showing perfect predictions.

Step 11: Decision tree regression is applied to predict product prices using specified features from the products dataset including margin percentage, shelf life days, minimum stock level, and maximum stock level. The decision tree regressor is configured with maximum depth to limit tree complexity and prevent overfitting, minimum samples split to ensure sufficient data at each split point, and random state for reproducibility. The tree recursively partitions the feature space by selecting splits that maximize variance reduction in the price target variable.

Step 12: After training the decision tree on product data, feature importances are extracted which quantify how much each feature contributes to reducing prediction error based on weighted impurity decrease across all tree nodes. The importance values are normalized to sum to one making them interpretable as relative contribution percentages. Analysis of the importance scores identifies which feature has the highest importance value, indicating the strongest determinant of product pricing.

Step 13: A horizontal bar chart is generated displaying the feature names on the y axis and their importance scores on the x axis. The features are sorted in ascending order of importance to highlight the most influential variables. This visualization helps product managers understand which inventory and product characteristics most influence pricing decisions and should be prioritized in pricing strategy.

Step 14: For Naive Bayes classification to predict delivery success, a binary target variable is created where one value represents on time deliveries and another represents late deliveries including delayed categories. The promised hour feature is extracted from the promised delivery time timestamp by taking the hour component to capture time of day effects on delivery performance. Customer order frequency is calculated by counting the number of orders per customer identifier to represent how familiar delivery partners may be with frequent customer addresses.

Step 15: The features are used to train a Gaussian Naive Bayes classifier which assumes each feature follows a normal distribution within each class. The Naive Bayes algorithm treats features as conditionally independent given the class label which simplifies probability calculations and enables efficient training. The model is evaluated on a validation set to measure classification accuracy for predicting on time versus late deliveries.

Step 16: A confusion matrix is computed by cross tabulating actual delivery status against predicted delivery status for the test set, showing counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. The confusion matrix is visualized as a heatmap with color intensity representing the count magnitude in each cell, predicted classes on the x axis, and actual classes on the y axis. This visualization reveals classification patterns including whether the model tends to overpredict certain classes or has balanced error rates across both classes.

Step 17: Granger causality testing examines whether historical marketing spend values help predict future revenue by creating two time series of daily spend and daily revenue aggregated from the marketing dataset. The test uses a specified maximum lag order meaning it checks whether spend values from previous days provide statistically significant predictive information about current day revenue. The F statistic is calculated by comparing restricted regression models without spend lags versus unrestricted models including spend lags to assess the causal relationship.

Step 18: Principal component analysis is applied to product features after standardizing them to zero mean and unit variance. PCA extracts a specified number of principal components which are uncorrelated linear combinations of the original features that capture maximum variance in the data. The variance explained by the first principal component is calculated representing the dominant pattern of variation across product characteristics, and these component scores are subsequently used as predictors in linear regression to predict maximum retail price.

Step 19: Partial least squares regression analyzes the relationship between marketing metrics and order totals by attempting to merge the orders dataset with the marketing dataset based on matching date fields. When the date based merge yields insufficient matching records, the implementation applies a sampling approach to create the analysis dataset. Marketing predictors are extracted along with order total as the target, and PLS regression with specified components finds latent variables maximizing covariance between predictors and outcome to produce regression coefficients.

Step 20: Bootstrap confidence interval estimation for the mean margin percentage draws multiple random samples with replacement from the observed margin values in the products dataset where each bootstrap sample has the same size as the original dataset. For each bootstrap sample the sample mean is calculated creating an empirical sampling distribution of the mean statistic. The confidence interval is constructed using the percentile method by taking specified percentiles as the lower and upper bounds, with the interval width quantifying uncertainty in the population mean estimate.

Step 21: Click through rate variance analysis calculates CTR for each marketing campaign as clicks divided by impressions multiplied by one hundred to express as a percentage, then groups campaigns by their channel field. Within each channel the variance of CTR values across all campaigns is computed to measure performance consistency, with higher variance indicating more fluctuation between high and low performing campaigns. The channel with maximum CTR variance is identified as exhibiting the most inconsistent click performance with substantial variability across different campaigns.

Step 22: Delivery time deviation analysis calculates the difference in minutes between actual delivery time and promised delivery time for each order by subtracting the timestamps and converting to total minutes. The absolute values of these time deviations are grouped by the delivery status field including different delay categories, and mean absolute deviation is computed for each status group. The range of mean absolute deviations is calculated as the maximum minus minimum across the status categories, representing the spread in average lateness between the most punctual and most delayed delivery categories.

Step 23: The prompt requires calculating product turnover ratio by merging products with aggregated order counts by product category which necessitates linking individual orders to specific products, but the orders dataset lacks product identifier making it impossible to determine which products were actually ordered. As a methodological workaround, the implementation applies a uniform distribution assumption where total orders are divided equally across all products regardless of actual purchasing patterns, then aggregated by product category to estimate demand volumes. This proxy method deviates from the prompt specification by using an invented uniform allocation rather than real order data, producing arbitrary turnover ratios that do not reflect actual inventory movement or category performance, with the category exhibiting maximum ratio identified based on this assumption driven calculation rather than empirical transaction records.
