QC NOTES FOR BLINKIT RETAIL ANALYTICS PROJECT

PROMPT GOAL:

The project performs comprehensive retail analytics on Blinkit datasets including 5000 order transactions, 268 product records, and 5400 marketing campaign records using advanced machine learning and statistical techniques to optimize inventory management and marketing return on investment. Time series forecasting with ARIMA predicts daily revenue patterns, while regression methods including k nearest neighbors, decision trees, and partial least squares model order values and product pricing relationships. Classification algorithms including Naive Bayes predict delivery success probability, and dimensionality reduction techniques using principal component analysis identify key pricing drivers. Statistical testing methods including Granger causality examine marketing spend effectiveness, bootstrap methods quantify confidence intervals for margin estimation, and variance analysis evaluates channel performance consistency. The analysis integrates demand forecasting, customer behavior modeling, delivery performance prediction, and marketing optimization to provide actionable insights for retail operations and strategic decision making.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTIONS:

Step 1: The analysis begins by loading three Excel files into pandas dataframes including blinkit orders with order transactions and delivery information, blinkit products with pricing and inventory specifications, and blinkit marketing performance with campaign metrics and revenue data. All required Python libraries are imported including statsmodels for time series analysis, scikit learn for machine learning algorithms, numpy for numerical computations, and matplotlib with seaborn for visualization generation. The data loading process validates that all columns are properly recognized including datetime fields for order and delivery timestamps, numeric fields for financial metrics, and categorical fields for status and channel classifications.

Step 2: For ARIMA time series forecasting, the marketing performance dataset is aggregated by date to calculate total daily revenue by grouping all campaign records by their date field and summing the revenue generated values. The resulting daily revenue series spans 600 unique dates and is sorted in chronological order to maintain temporal sequence which is essential for time series modeling. This aggregated daily revenue becomes the target variable for forecasting future revenue patterns using autoregressive integrated moving average methodology.

Step 3: The daily revenue time series is split into training and testing sets using an 80 percent to 20 percent ratio where the first 480 days are designated for model training and the remaining 120 days are held out for validation. This temporal split preserves the chronological ordering of observations ensuring that the model learns from historical data and is evaluated on future unseen data. The split methodology differs from random cross validation because time series analysis requires respecting temporal dependencies and avoiding data leakage from future to past.

Step 4: An ARIMA model is configured with order parameters p equals 2 for autoregressive terms, d equals 1 for first order differencing to achieve stationarity, and q equals 2 for moving average components. The p parameter of 2 means the model uses revenue values from the two most recent time points to predict the next value, capturing short term autocorrelation patterns. The d parameter of 1 applies differencing to remove trend and make the series stationary, while q equals 2 incorporates two lagged forecast errors to model the moving average structure.

Step 5: The fitted ARIMA model generates one step ahead forecasts for each of the 120 test period days by iteratively predicting the next day revenue value. Mean absolute error is calculated as the average of absolute differences between actual and forecasted revenues across all test days, quantifying the typical prediction error magnitude. The resulting MAE of 5282.67 dollars indicates that on average the model predictions deviate from actual daily revenues by approximately 5283 dollars, providing a metric for assessing forecast reliability.

Step 6: A line plot visualization is generated with date on the x axis and daily revenue on the y axis, displaying three series including training data in blue, actual test revenue in green, and forecasted revenue as a red dashed line. This visualization enables stakeholders to visually assess how well the ARIMA model captures revenue trends, seasonal patterns, and overall trajectory compared to observed values. The plot is saved as arima forecast png for documentation and reporting purposes.

Step 7: For k nearest neighbors regression to predict order totals, three customer level features are engineered by aggregating the orders dataset at the customer id level. Average order total per customer is calculated by grouping orders by customer id and computing the mean of order total values, capturing each customer typical spending level. Customer order count is calculated as the number of order records per customer id representing purchase frequency, and normalized order total is created by dividing each order total by the overall mean to standardize transaction scales.

Step 8: The three KNN features including average order total, order count, and normalized total are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using the StandardScaler transformer from scikit learn. Standardization is critical for k nearest neighbors because the algorithm computes Euclidean distances between observations and features with larger numeric ranges would disproportionately influence distance calculations. After standardization all features contribute equally to the similarity metric regardless of their original measurement scales.

Step 9: The standardized feature matrix and order total target variable are split into 80 percent training and 20 percent testing sets using random sampling to create independent validation data. A k nearest neighbors regressor is instantiated with k equals 7 neighbors and Euclidean distance metric, then fitted on the scaled training data. The choice of 7 neighbors balances between using too few neighbors which causes overfitting to noise and using too many neighbors which oversmooths patterns and reduces model flexibility.

Step 10: The trained KNN model generates predictions for the test set by finding the 7 nearest training observations to each test point and averaging their order total values. The R squared coefficient of determination is calculated comparing predicted versus actual order totals, yielding a value of 0.9969 which indicates that 99.69 percent of variance in order totals is explained by the customer features. A scatter plot is created with actual order totals on the x axis and predicted order totals on the y axis along with a diagonal reference line showing perfect predictions.

Step 11: Decision tree regression is applied to predict product prices using four features from the products dataset including margin percentage, shelf life days, minimum stock level, and maximum stock level. The decision tree regressor is configured with maximum depth of 6 levels to limit tree complexity and prevent overfitting, minimum samples split of 10 to ensure sufficient data at each split point, and random state 42 for reproducibility. The tree recursively partitions the feature space by selecting splits that maximize variance reduction in the price target variable.

Step 12: After training the decision tree on product data, feature importances are extracted which quantify how much each feature contributes to reducing prediction error based on weighted impurity decrease across all tree nodes. The importance values are normalized to sum to 1.0 making them interpretable as relative contribution percentages. Analysis of the importance scores identifies max stock level as the feature with the highest importance value, indicating that maximum inventory capacity is the strongest determinant of product pricing.

Step 13: A horizontal bar chart is generated displaying the four feature names on the y axis and their importance scores ranging from 0 to 1 on the x axis. The features are sorted in ascending order of importance with max stock level appearing at the top with the longest bar. This visualization helps product managers understand which inventory and product characteristics most influence pricing decisions and should be prioritized in pricing strategy.

Step 14: For Naive Bayes classification to predict delivery success, a binary target variable on time is created where 1 represents delivery status equals On Time and 0 represents late deliveries including Slightly Delayed and Significantly Delayed categories. The promised hour feature is extracted from the promised delivery time timestamp by taking the hour component to capture time of day effects on delivery performance. Customer order frequency is calculated by counting the number of orders per customer id to represent how familiar delivery partners may be with frequent customer addresses.

Step 15: The three features including promised hour, order total, and customer order frequency are used to train a Gaussian Naive Bayes classifier which assumes each feature follows a normal distribution within each class. The Naive Bayes algorithm treats features as conditionally independent given the class label which simplifies probability calculations and enables efficient training. Despite the independence assumption which may not strictly hold in practice, the model achieves 70.10 percent classification accuracy on the validation set for predicting on time versus late deliveries.

Step 16: A confusion matrix is computed by cross tabulating actual delivery status against predicted delivery status for the test set, showing counts of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. The confusion matrix is visualized as a heatmap with color intensity representing the count magnitude in each cell, predicted classes on the x axis, and actual classes on the y axis. This visualization reveals classification patterns including whether the model tends to overpredict on time deliveries or has balanced error rates across both classes.

Step 17: Granger causality testing examines whether historical marketing spend values help predict future revenue by creating two time series of daily spend and daily revenue aggregated from the marketing dataset. The test uses a maximum lag order of 5 meaning it checks whether spend values from up to 5 days prior provide statistically significant predictive information about current day revenue. The F statistic of 0.68 is calculated by comparing restricted regression models without spend lags versus unrestricted models including spend lags, with low F values suggesting weak evidence that spend Granger causes revenue at the 5 day lag.

Step 18: Principal component analysis is applied to five product features including price, margin percentage, shelf life days, minimum stock level, and maximum stock level after standardizing them to zero mean and unit variance. PCA extracts three principal components which are uncorrelated linear combinations of the original features that capture maximum variance in the data. The first principal component explains 23.39 percent of total variance representing the dominant pattern of variation across product characteristics, and these component scores are subsequently used as predictors in linear regression to predict maximum retail price.

Step 19: Partial least squares regression analyzes the relationship between marketing metrics and order totals by attempting to merge the orders dataset with the marketing dataset based on matching order date with campaign date fields. When the date based merge yields insufficient matching records, the implementation applies a random sampling approach selecting 1000 order records and 1000 marketing records independently to create the analysis dataset. Four marketing predictors including impressions, clicks, conversions, and ROAS are extracted along with order total as the target, and PLS regression with 2 components finds latent variables maximizing covariance between predictors and outcome yielding an impressions coefficient of negative 0.0042.

Step 20: Bootstrap confidence interval estimation for the mean margin percentage draws 1000 random samples with replacement from the observed margin values in the products dataset where each bootstrap sample has the same size as the original dataset. For each of the 1000 bootstrap samples the sample mean is calculated creating an empirical sampling distribution of the mean statistic. The 95 percent confidence interval is constructed using the percentile method by taking the 2.5th percentile as the lower bound and the 97.5th percentile as the upper bound, with the interval width of 1.81 percentage points quantifying uncertainty in the population mean estimate.

Step 21: Click through rate variance analysis calculates CTR for each marketing campaign as clicks divided by impressions multiplied by 100 to express as a percentage, then groups campaigns by their channel field including Social Media, Email, Search, and Display. Within each channel the variance of CTR values across all campaigns is computed to measure performance consistency, with higher variance indicating more fluctuation between high and low performing campaigns. The Email channel exhibits maximum CTR variance of 191.80 percentage squared indicating the most inconsistent click performance with substantial variability across different email marketing campaigns.

Step 22: Delivery time deviation analysis calculates the difference in minutes between actual delivery time and promised delivery time for each order by subtracting the timestamps and converting to total minutes. The absolute values of these time deviations are grouped by the delivery status field including On Time, Slightly Delayed, and Significantly Delayed categories, and mean absolute deviation is computed for each status group. The range of mean absolute deviations is calculated as the maximum minus minimum across the three status categories yielding 20.12 minutes, representing the spread in average lateness between the most punctual and most delayed delivery categories.

Step 23: Product turnover rate by category aggregates the products dataset by category field to calculate average maximum stock level and product count within each category including Baby Care, Cold Drinks and Juices, Dairy and Breakfast, and other segments. Total orders are distributed across products proportionally based on product counts per category to estimate category level demand volumes. The turnover ratio is computed as estimated category order count divided by average max stock level for that category, with Dairy and Breakfast exhibiting the maximum ratio of 7.23 indicating fastest inventory movement relative to storage capacity among all product categories.
