PROMPT GOAL IN 5 SENTENCES:

The analysis aims to apply twelve advanced machine learning and statistical techniques to three air quality datasets from Brasilia, Cairo, and Dubai containing hourly measurements of seven pollutants and AQI across 8760 observations per city. Each technique serves a specific analytical purpose ranging from mixture modeling and source separation to dimensionality reduction and predictive modeling to comprehensively understand atmospheric pollution patterns. The primary objective is to extract latent pollution structures, identify independent emission sources, quantify relationships between pollutant groups, and build robust prediction models for air quality forecasting. The analysis generates specific metrics including BIC values, kurtosis measures, correlation coefficients, communalities, prediction errors, and model performance scores to evaluate each technique quantitatively. Finally, the study compares canonical correlation analysis with factor analysis to determine which method better reveals multivariate pollution structure through direct assessment of cross-set relationships versus individual variable variance decomposition.

STEP BY STEP SOLUTIONS:

Step 1: The analysis begins by loading three Excel files containing air quality data for Cairo, Dubai, and Brasilia where each dataset has 8760 hourly observations representing a full year of continuous monitoring. Each file contains Date, City, and seven pollutant measurements including carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, ozone, fine particulate matter PM2.5, coarse particulate matter PM10, and the composite Air Quality Index. The datasets are verified to have consistent structure with ten columns each ensuring compatibility for subsequent multivariate analyses that require combining or comparing data across cities.

Step 2: For the first analysis using Cairo data, expectation maximization algorithm is applied to the bivariate distribution of PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations to identify distinct pollution regimes. The algorithm initializes three Gaussian mixture components representing low, moderate, and high pollution states with full covariance matrices allowing arbitrary elliptical distributions in the two-dimensional feature space. The iterative EM process alternates between expectation step computing component probabilities and maximization step updating parameters until the log-likelihood function converges within 200 iterations.

Step 3: After fitting the Gaussian mixture model, the Bayesian Information Criterion is extracted as a model selection metric that balances goodness of fit against model complexity by penalizing the number of parameters. The BIC value of 125688.09 quantifies the trade-off between accurately representing the three pollution regimes and avoiding overfitting with too many mixture components. A scatter plot is generated showing PM2.5 on x-axis and PM10 on y-axis with points colored by their most probable component assignment revealing three distinct clusters corresponding to different pollution states.

Step 4: The second analysis applies independent component analysis to Dubai data using four pollutants CO, NO2, SO2, and O3 as observed signals that are assumed to be linear mixtures of independent latent sources. The data is first standardized to zero mean and unit variance to ensure all pollutants contribute equally regardless of their original measurement scales. FastICA algorithm with logcosh nonlinearity measure extracts three maximally independent components representing distinct emission sources such as traffic, industrial, and natural processes.

Step 5: After ICA decomposition, the kurtosis of the first independent component is calculated to measure the heavy-tailedness of its distribution which indicates the presence of extreme pollution events. A kurtosis value of 2.2692 suggests the component has slightly lighter tails than a normal distribution indicating relatively moderate pollution variability from this source. The three independent components are visualized as time series lines showing how each latent pollution source varies across the 8760 hourly observations with different temporal patterns.

Step 6: The third analysis implements canonical correlation analysis by vertically stacking Brasilia and Cairo datasets to create a combined dataset with 17520 observations. Two pollutant sets are created where set X contains primary gaseous pollutants CO, NO2, and SO2 while set Y contains particulate matter PM2.5 and PM10. CCA finds linear combinations of each set called canonical variates that maximize the correlation between the two sets revealing how primary pollutants and particulates co-vary.

Step 7: The CCA model with two components identifies pairs of canonical variates where the first pair captures the maximum possible correlation between linear combinations of the two pollutant groups. The first canonical correlation coefficient of 0.8554 indicates a very strong positive linear relationship between the weighted combination of primary pollutants and the weighted combination of particulates. This high correlation suggests that when certain primary pollutants increase together in a specific pattern, particulate matter levels also increase in a corresponding pattern.

Step 8: A scatter plot displays the relationship between the first canonical variate of X against the first canonical variate of Y where each point represents an hourly observation from either Brasilia or Cairo. The strong linear trend with correlation 0.8554 demonstrates that CCA successfully identified the direction of maximum association between the two pollutant groups. This visualization reveals the multivariate structure where multiple pollutants interact together rather than examining pairwise correlations individually.

Step 9: Factor analysis is applied to Dubai data using all six pollutants CO, NO2, SO2, O3, PM2.5, and PM10 to extract three underlying latent factors representing common pollution drivers. The data is standardized before fitting and varimax rotation is applied to make factors more interpretable by maximizing variance of squared loadings within each factor. The rotated factor loadings matrix shows how much each pollutant loads on each of the three latent factors revealing patterns like traffic-related emissions or industrial sources.

Step 10: The communality for PM2.5 is computed as the sum of squared loadings across all three factors which represents the proportion of PM2.5 variance explained by the common factors. A communality of 0.8177 means that 81.77 percent of the variability in PM2.5 concentrations can be explained by the three latent pollution drivers. The remaining 18.23 percent represents unique variance specific to PM2.5 that is not shared with other pollutants or measurement error.

Step 11: A heatmap visualizes the factor loading matrix with pollutants as rows and factors as columns where cell colors indicate loading magnitudes from negative one to positive one. Strong positive or negative loadings indicate which pollutants are primarily influenced by which latent factors allowing interpretation of factor meanings. For example, if CO, NO2, and PM2.5 all load strongly on Factor 1, this might represent a traffic emission factor.

Step 12: Variational autoencoder regression is implemented for probabilistic AQI prediction using Cairo data where the first 4000 observations serve as training data. The encoder network with layers of size 32 and 16 compresses five pollutant inputs into an 8-dimensional latent space capturing essential features. The decoder network reconstructs the AQI prediction from the latent representation while the VAE training optimizes both reconstruction accuracy and latent space regularization through KL divergence.

Step 13: The VAE is trained for 50 epochs with batch size 64 and learning rate 0.001 where the custom loss function combines mean squared error for reconstruction and weighted KL divergence for latent space regularization. After training, predictions are generated on the remaining test observations and mean absolute percentage error is calculated to evaluate prediction accuracy. The MAPE of 20.82 percent indicates that on average the VAE predictions deviate from actual AQI values by about one-fifth of their magnitude.

Step 14: Lasso-LARS regression is applied to Brasilia data for efficient feature selection using the least angle regression algorithm that computes the entire regularization path. With alpha set to 0.1, the L1 penalty encourages sparsity by shrinking some coefficients exactly to zero effectively removing those features from the model. After fitting, five out of six pollutant predictors have non-zero coefficients indicating that five features are selected as important for AQI prediction while one is eliminated.

Step 15: Elastic Net with cross-validation combines Dubai and Cairo data to predict PM10 using five other pollutants as predictors with L1 ratio set to 0.5 balancing ridge and lasso penalties. Five-fold cross-validation systematically tries different alpha values to find the optimal regularization strength that minimizes prediction error on held-out folds. The selected optimal alpha of 3.3581 represents the regularization parameter that best balances model complexity and prediction accuracy on unseen data.

Step 16: Truncated SVD is applied to standardized Brasilia pollutant matrix to reduce dimensionality from six features to three principal components while retaining maximum variance. Unlike PCA, truncated SVD works directly on the data matrix using singular value decomposition which is computationally efficient for large datasets. The cumulative explained variance ratio of 0.9164 indicates that three components capture 91.64 percent of the total variability meaning dimensionality is reduced by half with less than 10 percent information loss.

Step 17: Non-negative matrix factorization decomposes Cairo pollution data into two additive components representing distinct pollution patterns where all values must be non-negative. The data is shifted to ensure non-negativity before applying NMF with nndsvd initialization for faster convergence. The reconstruction error of 1349.60 measured as Frobenius norm quantifies how well the two-component factorization approximates the original 8760 by 4 pollution matrix.

Step 18: Quadratic discriminant analysis classifies Dubai observations into high pollution when AQI exceeds 75 versus normal pollution using four pollutant predictors with class-specific covariance matrices. The data is split 70-30 for training and testing with random state 42 ensuring reproducibility. The F1 score of 0.7705 indicates good classification performance balancing precision and recall meaning the model correctly identifies high pollution events while minimizing false alarms.

Step 19: Kernel PCA applies radial basis function kernel with gamma 0.1 to capture non-linear relationships in Brasilia pollutant data extracting three principal components in the kernel-transformed feature space. Unlike linear PCA, kernel PCA can identify curved or complex patterns by implicitly mapping data to higher-dimensional space where linear relationships exist. The first component explains 54.38 percent of variance in kernel space indicating it captures the dominant non-linear pollution pattern.

Step 20: Ridge regression with cross-validation combines Cairo and Dubai datasets to predict AQI using all six pollutants where alpha values from 0.1 to 100 are tested via five-fold cross-validation. The ridge penalty prevents overfitting by shrinking coefficients without eliminating any features entirely maintaining all pollutants in the model. The R-squared value of 0.6497 indicates the model explains 64.97 percent of AQI variance demonstrating reasonably good predictive power across both cities combined.

Step 21: The final comparison evaluates canonical correlation analysis against factor analysis for revealing multivariate pollution structure. CCA with first correlation 0.8554 directly measures the maximum linear relationship between two distinct pollutant groups showing how they co-vary together. In contrast, factor analysis communality of 0.8177 only indicates how much of one variable PM2.5 is explained by latent factors without quantifying relationships between pollutant groups. Therefore, canonical correlation analysis better reveals multivariate structure by explicitly capturing cross-set associations rather than just explaining individual variable variance through common factors.
